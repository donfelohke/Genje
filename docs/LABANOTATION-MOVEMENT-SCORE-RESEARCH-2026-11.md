# GENJE — Labanotation, Movement Scores and Choreohacking

**Date:** 2026-11-18  
**Status:** RESEARCH / PRACTICE FRAMEWORK  
**Domain:** Embodied creative practice, choreography, movement documentation and choreohacking.

## 1. Why movement notation belongs here

GENJE includes dance, choreography, improvisation, movement research and choreohacking. A notation system can help document movement sequences, spatial relations, timing and the body parts involved. It can support transmission, rehearsal, analysis and creative transformation, but it does not replace embodied knowledge, performer interpretation, cultural context or the lived practice.

## 2. Labanotation orientation

For institutional orientation, consult [Ohio State University Department of Dance — Resources](https://dance.osu.edu/research/dnb/resources), the [Dance Notation Bureau](https://dancenotation.org/) and the [International Council of Kinetography Laban](https://ickl.org/).

- **Labanotation / Kinetography Laban:** a detailed symbolic movement notation tradition.
- **Motif Description / Motif Notation:** a selective representation of essential actions and stillness.
- **Laban Movement Analysis (LMA):** a framework for observing and describing Body, Space and Effort.
- **Bartenieff Fundamentals:** a related movement practice, not a synonym for Labanotation.

These are related but not interchangeable. Wikipedia may be used for discovery, not as a teaching authority.

## 3. GENJE application

Potential uses:
- document a movement phrase, rehearsal or choreography;
- preserve timing, sequence, direction, level, body-part involvement and spatial relationships;
- compare an authored movement score with a later performance while recording differences;
- explore movement-to-sound, rhythm, visuals or code as an authored creative mapping;
- use Motif-style abstraction for composition and improvisation where a full score would be unnecessarily prescriptive.

A notation score records selected movement information. It does not, by itself, capture the full cultural meaning, intention, affect, social context or lived experience of a performer.

## 4. Choreohacking protocol

For each work or experiment, record work identity and collaborators; cultural context and source; notation method and score scope; performance conditions; explicit computational mappings; rights and consent; rehearsal/performance differences; archive versions and reproduction limitations.

## 5. Ethical and cultural boundaries

- Do not infer identity, health, emotion, personality, intent or capability from movement notation or motion data.
- Do not rank performers, score bodies or treat one movement vocabulary as a universal standard.
- Do not turn a notation system developed in one historical and cultural context into a universal account of African movement.
- Preserve performer and community authorship, consent, credit, context and the right to refuse recording.
- Treat recordings, movement scores and identifiable performance data as potentially sensitive.
- No automated movement-recognition or motion-capture implementation is claimed by this document.

## 6. African dance documentation: comparative evidence

The research must not assume that a European-origin notation system is a universal or sufficient representation of African movement.

- Latipher Amma Osei (2020), *Documenting Ghanaian dances through Labanotation* (*African Research Review* 14(2), 112–120), reviews Labanotation as one possible safeguarding approach, discusses challenges in its use for African dances, and considers combining notation with cinematography. [University of Education, Winneba record](https://www.uew.edu.gh/theatre-arts/staff/laosei/publications/29001/detail).
- Felix Akinsipe (2015), *Development of 'Stick Figure' Notation for African Dances*, is a proposal in one scholarly work, not an established consensus or validated replacement for Labanotation. Full text review remains necessary.
- The Dance Notation Bureau's archive note on Odette Blum's *African Dances and Games* describes a publication combining dance style, cultural/historical background, rhythm, film and Labanotation. The archive restricts reuse; do not copy scores or images without rights clearance.
- Grim-Feinberg and Wong Santos, *Movement Notation and the Analysis of Embodied Knowledge: Three Ethnographic Examples*, is a methodological reference for practitioner correction, embodied interpretation and multi-modal teaching records. [Article](https://jashm.press.uillinois.edu/22.2/grim-feinberg.html).

## 7. Plural documentation protocol

For locally situated movement work, select a bundle with practitioners rather than imposing one format: practitioner account; embodied demonstration/learning where appropriate; audio; video/stills with consent; notation selected for the task; variation register; contextual annotations; community review; rights/access controls; and a preservation package linking source media, notation and metadata.

A score is a situated representation of selected aspects of a performance, not the performance itself.

## 8. Research priorities

- Review Osei (2020) in full, especially challenges identified for Labanotation in Ghanaian dance contexts.
- Retrieve and critically assess Akinsipe (2015) full text.
- Study the Dance Notation Bureau archive record without reproducing restricted material.
- Learn from collaborative transcription methods: practitioner correction, embodied interpretation and multi-modal records.
- Identify local practitioners and communities before defining any Turkana, Ateker or Genge-specific notation project.
- Compare documentation bundles for usefulness, consent, cultural fit and future community access.
