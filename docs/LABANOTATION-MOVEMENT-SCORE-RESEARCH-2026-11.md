# GENJE — Labanotation, Movement Scores and Choreohacking

**Date:** 2026-11-09  
**Status:** RESEARCH / PRACTICE FRAMEWORK  
**Domain:** Embodied creative practice, choreography, movement documentation and choreohacking.

## 1. Why movement notation belongs here

GENJE includes dance, choreography, improvisation, movement research and choreohacking. A notation system can help document movement sequences, spatial relations, timing and the body parts involved. It can support transmission, rehearsal, analysis and creative transformation, but it does not replace embodied knowledge, performer interpretation, cultural context or the lived practice.

## 2. Labanotation orientation

The supplied [Wikipedia Labanotation overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labanotation) is a discovery source, not a teaching authority. It describes a symbolic staff system associated with Rudolf Laban and subsequent practitioners.

For an institutional orientation, consult [Ohio State University Department of Dance — Resources](https://dance.osu.edu/research/dnb/resources). Its overview distinguishes:
- **Labanotation / Kinetography Laban:** a detailed symbolic movement notation tradition;
- **Motif Description / Motif Notation:** a selective representation of essential actions and stillness;
- **Laban Movement Analysis (LMA):** a framework for observing and describing Body, Space and Effort.

These are related but not interchangeable. Bartenieff Fundamentals is another related movement practice, not a synonym for Labanotation.

## 3. GENJE application

Potential uses:
- document a movement phrase, rehearsal or choreography;
- preserve timing, sequence, direction, level, body-part involvement and spatial relationships;
- compare an authored movement score with a later performance while recording differences;
- explore movement-to-sound, rhythm, visuals or code as an authored creative mapping;
- use Motif-style abstraction for composition and improvisation where a full score would be unnecessarily prescriptive.

A notation score records selected movement information. It does not, by itself, capture the full cultural meaning, intention, affect, social context or lived experience of a performer.

## 4. Choreohacking protocol

For each work or experiment, record:
1. **Work identity:** title, author, collaborators, date and maturity label.
2. **Cultural context:** dance form, community, language, place, lineage or source material where known; mark unknowns.
3. **Movement source:** original improvisation, commissioned choreography, public-domain score, licensed material or other documented origin.
4. **Notation method:** Labanotation, Kinetography Laban, Motif, LMA vocabulary, video annotation or an explicitly named hybrid.
5. **Score scope:** what is recorded, what is omitted and why.
6. **Performance conditions:** space, orientation, timing, music, props, participants and adaptations.
7. **Computational mapping:** explicit mapping from movement feature to sound, visual or code behaviour; label it as an artistic choice, not an objective equivalence.
8. **Rights and consent:** score rights, music/sample licences, performer consent, recording permissions and distribution conditions.
9. **Rehearsal and comparison:** record what changed between score, rehearsal and performance; preserve the performer's account.
10. **Archive:** source files, versions, notation format, documentation and reproduction limitations.

## 5. Ethical and cultural boundaries

- Do not infer identity, health, emotion, personality, intent or capability from movement notation or motion data.
- Do not rank performers, score bodies or treat one movement vocabulary as a universal standard.
- Do not turn a notation system developed in one historical and cultural context into a universal account of African movement.
- Preserve performer and community authorship, consent, credit, context and the right to refuse recording.
- Treat recordings, movement scores and identifiable performance data as potentially sensitive.
- No automated movement-recognition or motion-capture implementation is claimed by this document.

## 6. Research questions

- What is gained and lost when a living movement phrase is transcribed?
- When is detailed Labanotation useful, and when is a more open Motif description better?
- How do performers annotate, correct, contest or reinterpret a score?
- How can Genge-rooted movement and locally situated choreographic vocabularies be documented without forcing them into imported categories?
- What forms of computational mapping preserve performer agency and creative ambiguity?

## 7. African dance documentation: comparative evidence

The research must not assume that a European-origin notation system is a universal or sufficient representation of African movement. The available literature suggests a more careful, plural method.

### Ghanaian scholarship
Latipher Amma Osei's 2020 article, *Documenting Ghanaian dances through Labanotation* (*African Research Review* 14(2), 112–120), reviews Labanotation as one possible safeguarding approach, discusses challenges in its use for African dances, and considers combining notation with cinematography. The University of Education, Winneba record also describes oral, aural and observational transmission as existing preservation practices.

### Nigerian notation proposal
Felix Akinsipe's 2015 University of Ilorin repository abstract, *Development of 'Stick Figure' Notation for African Dances*, argues for developing a notation approach responsive to African dance and identifies timing and direction as problems to address. This is a proposal in one scholarly work, not an established consensus or validated replacement for Labanotation. Full text review is still required.

### Historical Ghana / Nigeria archive
The Dance Notation Bureau's 2026 archive note on Odette Blum's *African Dances and Games* describes fieldwork after her 1966–1968 teaching period at the University of Ghana. The publication reportedly combines dance style, cultural/historical background, rhythm, film and Labanotation; examples are Ghanaian except Agide from Nigeria. The DNB explicitly restricts the PDF to educational/research use and prohibits sharing, reproducing or selling it without permission. Do not copy its scores or images into GENJE without rights clearance.

### Ethnographic method lesson
Grim-Feinberg and Wong Santos' article, *Movement Notation and the Analysis of Embodied Knowledge: Three Ethnographic Examples*, is a particularly important methodological reference. In their Lahu Na Shehleh case, initial scores made from video were revised with a Lahu dancer, who corrected phrase boundaries, counts, mistakes, stylistic details and movement interpretations. The article reports that audio and video were needed alongside notation for teaching. This is strong evidence for co-authorship and contextual inquiry, not for replacing local knowledge with a notation expert's transcription.

## 8. GENJE's plural documentation protocol

For a locally situated movement work, use a bundle selected with the practitioners rather than a single imposed format:

1. **Practitioner account:** names, context, language, social purpose and meanings as the knowledge holders describe them.
2. **Demonstration and embodied learning:** where appropriate, researchers learn by participating or observing with permission; do not rely solely on distant video interpretation.
3. **Audio:** musical structure, rhythmic cues, speech, call-and-response and environmental sound where permitted.
4. **Video / stills:** spatial and temporal reference under explicit recording consent; respect no-camera contexts.
5. **Movement notation:** choose Labanotation, Motif, diagrammatic or locally authored notation only where useful and accepted.
6. **Variation register:** record variants and disagreements rather than forcing a single canonical version.
7. **Contextual annotations:** costume, props, floor, orientation, participants, audience, event, season and other relevant conditions.
8. **Community review:** practitioners review, correct, annotate, restrict or reject the documentation.
9. **Rights and access:** record authorship, permissions, attribution, access level, retention and withdrawal conditions.
10. **Preservation package:** store source media, notation, metadata and version history separately with clear relationships.

A score should be treated as a situated representation of selected aspects of a performance, not the performance itself.

## 9. GENJE research priorities

- Review Osei (2020) in full, especially the challenges identified for Labanotation in Ghanaian dance contexts.
- Retrieve and critically assess Akinsipe (2015) full text before evaluating the stick-figure proposal.
- Study the DNB Blum archive record without reproducing restricted material.
- Learn from the Lahu collaborative transcription method: practitioner correction, embodied interpretation and multi-modal teaching records.
- Identify local practitioners and communities before defining any Turkana, Ateker or Genge-specific notation project.
- Compare documentation bundles for usefulness, consent, cultural fit and future community access.

**Research boundary:** no universal African dance notation, locally validated Turkana notation, community-approved archive, or GENJE motion-capture implementation is claimed.
