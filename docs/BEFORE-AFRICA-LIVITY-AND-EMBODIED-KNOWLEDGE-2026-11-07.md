# Before Africa — Livity, Rhythm and Embodied Knowledge

**GENJE research note · Updated 11 November 2026 · Status: RESEARCH**

## Premise

Human knowledge is not transmitted only through written text or formal institutions. Sound, rhythm, dance, gesture, craft, oral performance, apprenticeship and collective memory can carry knowledge. This research asks how particular African communities have created, embodied, transmitted and transformed knowledge through performance and everyday practice.

The parent research programme is the FELOH Sovereign Corpus *Before Africa* dossier, which situates naming, population history, technology and political life before the dominance of the continental label “Africa.”

## Livity: use with historical precision

“Livity” is especially associated with Rastafari thought and practice in Jamaica. GENJE may use it as a contemporary interpretive lens for vitality, embodied agency, ethical life, spiritual orientation and self-determination. Do not present it as an ancient pan-African word or a universal indigenous philosophy. Research each community's own concepts and vocabulary in context.

Likewise, “Black” is a historically changing identity and political formation, not one timeless category shared identically across all African societies. The corpus dossier maintains the distinction between deep African ancestry/heritage and modern racial classification.

## Research strands

- **Oral knowledge:** praise poetry, narrative, proverb, genealogy, testimony, public memory and specialist recitation.
- **Sound and rhythm:** instruments, tuning, rhythmic structures, work songs, ritual sound, social dance and musical exchange.
- **Movement:** gesture, dance, martial and work movement, initiation, celebration and embodied pedagogy (including Laban-informed observation and notation as contemporary research tools).
- **Craft knowledge:** making, repair, material selection, apprenticeship, pattern and aesthetic judgement.
- **Collective memory:** how performance preserves, revises or contests histories and identities.
- **Ecological and technological knowledge:** links to the Green Sahara, early pottery, pastoral adaptation and regional subsistence systems documented in the continental atlas brief.

## GENJE practice connection

Genge, Genge Algo, livecoding, TidalCycles, SuperCollider, Hydra, Odi+ and Laban-informed movement are contemporary creative practices and tools. They can enter dialogue with historical and living traditions through careful attribution, collaboration and consent—not by claiming direct continuity without evidence or sampling cultural material without permission.

Document the artist, community, location, language, date, collaborators, source, permissions, context, transformations and limits. Distinguish research, prototype, performance and released work. Respect sacred, restricted and community-held knowledge.

## Working questions

1. What knowledge is encoded in rhythm, gesture, song, dance and craft?
2. Who is authorised to transmit or interpret it?
3. How do performance and oral traditions preserve and revise memory?
4. How have urbanisation, migration, recording technology and popular music transformed practice?
5. How can contemporary computational art engage living traditions without flattening them?
6. How do embodied practices relate to the deeper population, environmental and technological histories recovered in the Before Africa programme?
